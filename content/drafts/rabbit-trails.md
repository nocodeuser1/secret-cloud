# Additions and rabbit trails

None of this was said on the call. These are my additions as your build partner, for the two of you and the group to argue with. Size is build effort: S days, M weeks, L a season.

## Rabbit trails

### A. Walk with your ears

Put the phone in your pocket. Each drop sits in the stereo field where it really is, so a loop on your left plays louder in your left ear. Turn toward it and it swells to center. Walk away and it thins out. A soft haptic tap when you cross into a radius says something is here, no glance needed. No arrows, no blue dot. The map stays optional. Plain stereo panning and distance curves can do all of this.

**Why it fits:** phone leads you there, then gets out of the way.

**Size:** M

### B. The violin at the rock

Israel's perfect spot, taken one step further for the people who want it. With AirPods and head tracking, each instrument gets a fixed position in the park on top of its gain curve. Turn your head and the violin stays at the rock. Walk around it and it moves behind you. The perfect spot becomes a real place where the stereo image lines up. Keep it for soundscapes only, so they stay special. Everyone else gets the plain louder-as-you-approach mix.

**Why it fits:** the instrument sits where it was played.

**Size:** L

### C. Dusk version, rain version

The same soundscape, with layers that only open at certain hours or weather. At dusk the pads get longer and a quieter loop fades in under the mix. When it rains, a different take of the same song plays, recorded on a rainy day. The phone clock and a free weather feed are all it takes. No new content at first; use alternate takes you already have. People who walked it at noon come back at dusk to hear what changed.

**Why it fits:** same park, new experience; the place brings you back.

**Size:** S

### D. Leave a stone

A listener at a drop can leave a voice note, twenty seconds, tied to that spot. Nothing goes live until a curator listens and approves it. Approved notes play faintly under the drop for later walkers, like someone whispering a few feet away. Slowly a spot gathers a quiet pile of voices: a thank you, life spoken over someone, a line someone needed to say. This is the reply and testimony idea, moved out of the inbox and into the ground.

**Why it fits:** replies live in the place; the curator keeps the filter.

**Size:** M

### E. Breadcrumbs through the park

Some drops stay silent until you have heard another one first. Stand at the first for its full length and the second wakes up. Follow the chain and the last drop is the easter egg on the stone. Nobody is told the order. You find out by noticing a spot that was quiet last week is now playing. A soundscape can mix free-roaming drops with one hidden trail. It gives the monthly drop a shape: this month's trail starts at the gate.

**Why it fits:** a trail for the right person; the phone only hums.

**Size:** S

### F. Paper first, then phone

Before the app has a sign-up screen, it has a poster. Risograph prints in two inks, a cloud shape, no band name, one QR code. Hand-stamped cards left in coffee shops and tucked into library books. The card is the invitation; scanning it is the only way in. No app store search, no link in a bio. The first contact is physical and strange, which is the point. Each print run carries its own code, so you learn which cards found people.

**Why it fits:** this is the sign on the street.

**Size:** S

### G. The park never repeats

Instead of one fixed loop per drop, give a drop a small set of your own loops and a few rules: which ones can play together, how long each waits before it returns, which key they share. Every visit, the drop picks a fresh combination. Same stems, same vibe, different morning. No generated audio; the rules do the shuffling and you recorded every note. Ten loops with simple rules make hundreds of versions, and the second visit never matches the first.

**Why it fits:** a place that never repeats keeps the right person returning.

**Size:** M

### H. A living map

The regional archive, drawn as a map instead of a list. Every secret show leaves a pin: where, when, and the recording for the people who were there. Every soundscape leaves a softer mark for each month it ran. Zoom out and you see where it has happened. Zoom in on a town and you see its history. Travelers use it the way Israel described, to know where to look. Nothing on it is a count, only dates and places.

**Why it fits:** the heat map, about places and never numbers.

**Size:** M

### I. The show without screens

Three ways to do the in-show experience without anyone staring at a phone. First, a slow light field: every phone in the radius dims to one color and breathes with the set, face up on the grass. Second, one shared amen: at a point the band chooses, every phone buzzes once. Third, nothing during the show, only the reward afterward, the recording arriving in your pocket as you walk to the car. My vote is the third. Let people look at the band.

**Why it fits:** the phone stays dark; the reward is for whoever came.

**Size:** S

### J. One iPad in the woods

A kit for the woods. One iPad runs the loops and acts as the amp. The Microcosm's job goes to a granular app, one less box and one less cable. A small bus-powered interface, guitar and a vocal mic into it. A battery PA, six hours, backpack size. A blanket. Everything one person carries in. I would write the signal chain and app list once we test it in the park at Guthrie, then post it as the kit any curator can copy.

**Why it fits:** the band in the woods, with nothing in the way.

**Size:** S

### K. Trusted friends run clouds

The group is the first curator and should not be the only one. A curator is a person or band account trusted to place drops, run shows, approve stones, and answer replies. New curators come in by invitation from an existing one, like listeners. Each cloud has its own name, park, and monthly rhythm. A friend in another town can run theirs without asking you. You two keep the Guthrie cloud and set the standard for what a curator is.

**Why it fits:** curators choose what to say; the public app never preaches.

**Size:** M

### L. The only number we track

The mission filter is "find the right person, not the most people", so track one thing: how many people came back to the same place a second month. Downloads, scans, and listens stay private. No feed, because a feed rewards posting. No likes, because likes reward being seen. No public counts, because counts make people compare. Invitations only, because an invitation means somebody chose you. Every feature gets one question: does this help one person come back, or more people show up once?

**Why it fits:** the mission filter, turned into something you can measure.

**Size:** S

## Trails I'd leave alone

**A web listening room.** A page that plays the park to a couch is easy to build, and it undoes the whole idea, because a couch has no place. If someone cannot get to Guthrie, the answer is a curator near them.

**Crowd-controlled effects at a show.** Twelve people tapping a filter sweep at once sounds like twelve people tapping a filter sweep, and Israel already called it a probable disaster. It also turns every face in the radius down toward a screen.

**Leaderboards, streaks, and badges.** They work, which is the problem: people would come to keep a streak alive and stop noticing the stone. Once a count is public, the right person starts comparing themselves to the most people.

## Where I'd steer it

Start with soundscapes only. One park, Guthrie. Five to ten loops, placed by hand, with one perfect spot and one easter egg on the stone. No band account yet, no QR codes, no shows. The first listeners are the group. Walk them through it on a Saturday, then ask what they heard. That is the first release, and it should exist in weeks.

Secret shows come second, because a show needs a crowd mechanism: the QR code, the invitation, the radius, the directions, the reward. None of it is tested until strangers follow a sign. Soundscapes work with two people and a park. Get the listening right first, and the show side inherits a map, an audio engine, and people who already trust the thing.

The bigger frame: the product is a platform for curated listening places. The group is its first curator, and the park in Guthrie is its first place. Build everything as if a friend in another town will run their own cloud next year. Drops belong to a curator. Replies go to the curator. The archive is kept per place. The kit recipe is a page anyone can copy.

Two rules I will not bend on. The public app never preaches. The place does the talking, and the curators choose what it says, which keeps the prophetic words and the worship exactly where you put them. And curators own their content: their loops, their replies, their testimonies, and the right to take any of it down.

Ship small. Walk the park every month, with whoever is new. Change one thing each time. Read every reply. When someone writes back that they felt the presence of God at the stone, that is the only signal I would trust. Let the testimonies tell you what to build next.
