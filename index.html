Where this came fromWhere this came from

One phone call in October, two ideas stacked on top of each other, and a name.

It started on a phone call between the two of you. I have grouped what was said by topic, in my own order. The idea came from Kellen: about fifty audio loops in one vibe, placed at spots in a real place like the park in Guthrie, each with a radius and a level at each ring, so that with no map at all you could follow your ears. The space is curated.

Then the easter eggs: a very tight radius on a special object, like a stone on top of a hill, so people wonder what that stone is saying, walk up, and hear something only there. THE VISION

A sound you can walk intoA sound you can walk into

Five pillars, all from the call, and a walk through the park as I imagine it.

Vote on each pillar, and leave a note where you would say it differently.

Drops and soundscapes

A drop is one piece of audio placed at a spot. It has a radius, say thirty feet, a decibel level at the outer ring and another at the inner ring. Fifty loops in one vibe across a park, and the space is curated. You could need no map at all, and follow your ears.

A soundscape is Israel's layer on top. Each instrument's gain is tied to a location. There is a perfect spot where the mix lines up the way it sounds on the album, in stereo. Walk toward the rock where the violin was played and the violin gets louder. It stays simple: louder as you approach. It is not true 3D audio. Instruments show as symbols on the map. Only soundscapes get this, so they stay special. The live jams and worship sessions are content too.

Easter eggs

Put a very tight radius on one special object. A stone on top of a hill. People notice it and wonder what that stone is saying. They walk up, and they hear something only there.

Those tight drops could be where the quiet things go: Bible verses, prophetic words, life spoken over someone. A ministry Israel was part of used to write hundreds of prophetic words a few times a year. The group could write its own. The stone says it, to whoever climbs the hill.

Secret shows from a band account

The app generates a QR code for the band. You post it around: flyers, signs, online. Scanning it is the invite. When someone shows up, their phone gets an invitation to join the event locally, with turn-by-turn directions to the radius. A scavenger hunt for music. Live performances only.

Inside the radius during the show, they can enter an app experience with the band. There were two ideas: letting the crowd alter the effects, which came up on the call as a probable disaster with many people, or visuals on loop that match the vibe of that area, generated monthly with a few hours of AI video work. There is a reward for showing up that nobody else gets, like a recording of that live set. Follow the band in the app and get notified each month.

Replies and testimonies

A listener can reply to whoever set up the soundscape. Nothing in the app announces itself as religious. So a reply is just a reply. And when someone feels the presence of God, their testimony comes in the same way.

It's all under the cover.

on the call

That is why the cover matters. The public app never preaches. The place does the talking, and the curators choose what to say. My gloss: lead with a label and the people you are looking for stay on the path. Under the cover, they walk off it, hear something, and write back.

Monthly drops and the archive

Once a space is designed, you can drop a new experience into the same park every month. People keep coming back, and a place becomes a habit. A city might love it because it brings people to the park.

Every secret show adds to the content and to a regional archive. A heat map shows travelers where most of this happens, so someone on a journey knows where to look.

How it feels

My addition: nobody described a walk on the call. I imagined one from what you said.

Saturday, a little after two. The park in Guthrie is mostly empty. A dog barks behind the pavilion, and the wind pushes dry leaves across the path.

There is a plain printed sign zip-tied to the post by the trailhead. Small, with the name and a short link on it. I type the link into my phone. The screen shows one line. Earbuds in, phone in my pocket. I do not look at it again for a while.

At first there is nothing. Gravel and wind. Then, a few steps on, a pad. Low, slow, one chord that keeps opening. Faint, off to the left. Two steps toward it and it gets a little stronger. Two steps back and it fades. So I leave the path.

The grass is long here and still damp in the shade. Under a hackberry the pad is fuller, and there is something else in it, a voice, far off, singing without words. I walk toward where it feels thickest. Twigs underfoot. The sound pulls me left, then up a slight rise.

And then it lines up. One more step and a violin comes in on the right, clean, the pad in the middle, the voice wide on the left, and I am standing inside a song. It sounds the way a record sounds, except there is a tree in front of me and a plane going over. I stay longer than I mean to.

When I look around, there is a stone at the top of the rise. Flat, grey, a little bigger than a loaf of bread. I walk to it because I want to know what it is saying.

I step up beside it and the music drops back, and a voice I do not know speaks a few slow lines, close, like someone standing next to me. My hand is on the stone. It is warm from the sun.

Then a branch snaps behind me. Someone in a grey hoodie comes through the same gap in the trees, earbuds in, listening. They stop where the violin comes in, and I watch their face change.

Things that must stay true

  • The public app never preaches. The place does the talking. Curators choose what to say.
  • It is about finding the right person. Reach is the wrong measure. No feeds, no public counts, no likes.
  • The phone leads you there, then gets out of the way.
  • Same park, new experience every month, so a place becomes a habit.

RABBIT TRAIL

Additions and rabbit trailsAdditions and rabbit trails

The ideas below are mine; some build on things said on the call. Twelve to argue with, and three I would leave alone.

These are my additions, for the two of you and the group to argue with. Each one says why it fits what you said and carries a size: S is days, M is weeks, L is a season. Vote on each of the twelve.

A

Walk with your ears

size M

Put the phone in your pocket. Each drop sits in the stereo field where it really is, so a loop on your left plays louder in your left ear. Turn toward it and it swells to center. Walk away and it thins out. A soft haptic tap when you cross into a radius says something is here. No arrows, no blue dot. Plain stereo panning and distance curves do all of this.

Why it fitsPhone leads you there, then gets out of the way.

B

The violin at the rock

size L

Israel's perfect spot, taken one step further. With AirPods and head tracking, each instrument gets a fixed position in the park on top of its gain curve. Turn your head and the violin stays at the rock. Walk around it and it moves behind you. The perfect spot becomes a real place where the stereo image lines up. Soundscapes only, so they stay special. Everyone else gets the plain mix.

Why it fitsThe instrument sits where it was played.

C

Dusk version, rain version

size S

The same soundscape, with layers that only open at certain hours or weather. At dusk the pads get longer and a quieter loop fades in under the mix. When it rains, a different take of the same song plays, recorded on a rainy day. The phone clock and a free weather feed are all it takes. No new content at first; use alternate takes you already have. People who walked it at noon come back at dusk.

Why it fitsSame park, new experience; the place brings you back.

D

Leave a stone

size M

A listener at a drop can leave a voice note, twenty seconds, tied to that spot. Nothing goes live until a curator listens and approves it. Approved notes play faintly under the drop for later walkers, like someone whispering a few feet away. Slowly a spot gathers a quiet pile of voices: a thank you, life spoken over someone, a line someone needed to say. The reply and testimony idea, moved out of the inbox and into the ground.

Why it fitsReplies live in the place; the curator keeps the filter.

E

Breadcrumbs through the park

size S

Some drops stay silent until you have heard another one first. Stand at the first for its full length and the second wakes up. Follow the chain and the last drop is the easter egg on the stone. Nobody is told the order. You notice that a spot quiet last week is now playing. A soundscape can mix free-roaming drops with one hidden trail, and it gives the monthly drop a shape: this month's trail starts at the gate.

Why it fitsA trail for the right person; the phone only hums.

F

Paper first, then phone

size S

Before the app has a sign-up screen, it has a poster. Risograph prints in two inks, a cloud shape, no band name, one QR code. Hand-stamped cards left in coffee shops and tucked into library books. The card is the invitation; scanning it is the only way in. No app store search, no link in a bio. The first contact is physical and strange. Each print run carries its own code, so you learn which cards found people.

Why it fitsThis is the sign on the street.

G

The park never repeats

size M

Instead of one fixed loop per drop, give a drop a small set of your own loops and a few rules: which ones can play together, how long each waits before it returns, which key they share. Every visit, the drop picks a fresh combination. Same stems, same vibe, different morning. No generated audio; the rules do the shuffling and you recorded every note. Ten loops with simple rules make hundreds of versions.

Why it fitsA place that never repeats keeps the right person returning.

H

A living map

size M

The regional archive, drawn as a map instead of a list. Every secret show leaves a pin: where, when, and the recording for the people who were there. Every soundscape leaves a softer mark for each month it ran. Zoom out and you see where it has happened. Zoom in on a town and you see its history. Travelers use it to know where to look. Nothing on it is a count, only dates and places.

Why it fitsThe heat map, about places and never numbers.

I

The show without screens

size S

Three ways to do the in-show experience without anyone staring at a phone. A slow light field: every phone in the radius dims to one color and breathes with the set, face up on the grass. One shared amen: at a point the band chooses, every phone buzzes once. Or nothing during the show, only the reward afterward, arriving in your pocket as you walk to the car. My vote is the third. Let people look at the band.

Why it fitsThe phone stays dark; the reward is for whoever came.

J

One iPad in the woods

size S

Israel's iPad rig, written down as a kit: one iPad runs the loops and replaces the amps with amp modeling. The Microcosm's granular job goes to an app on the same iPad, one less box and one less cable. I would add a small bus-powered interface, with guitar and a vocal mic into it. A battery PA, six hours, backpack size. A blanket. One person carries it all in. After we test it in Guthrie, I would post the signal chain and app list as the kit any curator can copy.

Why it fitsThe band in the woods, with nothing in the way.

K

Trusted friends run clouds

size M

The group is the first curator and should not be the only one. A curator is a person or band account trusted to place drops, run shows, approve stones, and answer replies. New curators come in by invitation from an existing one, like listeners. Each cloud has its own name, park, and monthly rhythm. A friend in another town can run theirs without asking you. You two keep the Guthrie cloud and set the standard.

Why it fitsCurators choose what to say; the public app never preaches.

L

The only number we track

size S

The mission filter is find the right person, not the most people, so track one thing: how many people came back to the same place a second month. Keep it private and never per person: the phone remembers the parks it has walked and sends one anonymous yes when it returns. No feed, because a feed rewards posting. No likes, because likes reward being seen. No public counts, because counts make people compare. Invitations only, because an invitation means somebody chose you. One question for every feature: does it help one person come back, or more people show up once?

Why it fitsThe mission filter, turned into something you can measure.

MY TAKE

Where I'd steer itWhere I'd steer it

Start with the listening. Let the shows inherit it.

Start with soundscapes only. One park, Guthrie. Five to ten loops, placed by hand, with one perfect spot and one easter egg on the stone. No band account yet, no QR codes, no shows. The first listeners are the group. Walk them through it on a Saturday, then ask what they heard. That is the first release, and it should exist in weeks.

Secret shows come second, because a show needs a crowd mechanism: the QR code, the invitation, the radius, the directions, the reward. None of it is tested until strangers follow a sign. Soundscapes work with two people and a park. Get the listening right first, and the show side inherits a map, an audio engine, and people who already trust the thing.

The bigger frame, my addition: the product is a home for curated listening places. The group is its first curator, and the park in Guthrie is its first place. Build everything as if a friend in another town will run their own cloud next year. Drops belong to a curator. Replies go to the curator. The archive is kept per place. The kit is a page anyone can copy.

Two rules I would hold to. The public app never preaches; the place does the talking, and the curators choose what it says, which keeps the prophetic words and the worship exactly where you put them. And curators own their content: their loops, their replies, their testimonies, and the right to take any of it down.

Ship small. Walk the park every month, with whoever is new. Change one thing each time. Read every reply. When someone writes back that they felt the presence of God at the stone, that is the signal I would trust most. Let the testimonies tell you what to build next.

GROUNDED

What is possible, in orderWhat is possible, in order

Four phases, the data, the math, and the parts of the phone that will fight us.

What is actually possible, in what order, and with what. The shape comes from the call: drops with a radius and two levels, the perfect spot, the QR code and the band account, the reward, monthly drops, the archive, the heat map and replies. The rest is mine: the order, the weeks on each phase, the stack, the data model, the math, the costs and the platform choices.

0

Prove the feeling

2 to 3 weeks

A web page, opened on a phone in the park in Guthrie, with five to ten of your loops placed at spots on the grass. The page reads your position with the browser's Geolocation API and plays the loops with the Web Audio API. Gain follows distance. The screen stays on through the Screen Wake Lock API. iOS Safari stops a web page's audio and location once the screen locks or Safari goes to the background, and no setting changes this. So Phase 0 is for walking with the phone in your hand, to feel what thirty feet means on real ground. On the first walk I also want to check (my addition) whether the mute switch silences Web Audio, and how long the first GPS fix takes under trees.

Deliverable
A shareable link the group walks with.
Risk
GPS under the Guthrie trees is rough enough that the loops feel random, and the radii have to grow.
1

The real walk

6 to 10 weeks

My addition: I picked every tool named in this phase. A native iOS app in Swift and SwiftUI. CoreLocation with when-in-use permission plus the background location mode, so the app keeps reading your position after you lock the phone and pocket it. AVAudioEngine with the audio background mode, so the loops keep playing. Beside it, a curator tool as a web page: a map (MapLibre with OpenStreetMap tiles, or Apple MapKit JS), drop a pin, set the radius and the two levels, upload the audio. The backend is Supabase: Postgres with PostGIS for region queries and later the archive and the heat map, Storage for audio, Auth for curators. The near-me check stays on the phone: it downloads the cloud's drop list and measures each distance itself. Apple expects a clear in-app reason for background location before the prompt, and real audio playing in the background. Battery: an hour of GPS at walking pace is fine, a whole afternoon is not.

Deliverable
A TestFlight build the group walks with, phone in pocket, plus a curator page to place drops.
Risk
App Store review asks us to justify background location, and we lose a week to it.
2

Secret shows

4 to 6 weeks

Band accounts. The app generates a QR code for a show and the band posts it. Scanning opens the app if you have it, or a page that gets you to it. A show has a time window, a spot and a radius. When your phone is inside the radius during the window, it gets the invitation to join. My addition: you accept with one tap, I'm here. That tap unlocks the in-show screen, and later the reward, for example a recording of that set. Directions to the radius hand off to Apple Maps (my addition). Follow a band and get a push each month. The push goes through APNs, which needs a small sender on Supabase (my addition).

Deliverable
A band can schedule a show, print the code, and the people who stood in the radius get the recording.
Risk
People scan the code at home, expect the show to be in the app, and find only a time and a direction.
3

The cloud grows

ongoing

A replies and testimonies inbox for curators. My addition: every reply waits for the curator before anyone else hears it, and approving one is a single tap. Monthly drop scheduling: a soundscape gets a publish date, so the same park wakes up new each month without anyone tapping deploy at midnight. A regional archive: every show adds its place and its recording, and the heat map for travelers is drawn from show locations, never from people. Then Android (my addition): a second app in Kotlin with its own rules for background location and its own store review. It shares the backend, so nothing on the curator side changes.

Deliverable
A curator plans the next three months of a park from the inbox, and the first Android walker joins.
Risk
The heat map starts to look like a count, and counts are what you said you do not want.

The data model

My addition. The names are yours.

  • clouds: id, name, owner, created_at. A curator space.
  • drops: id, cloud_id, soundscape_id (optional), audio_path, lat, lng, radius_m, inner_db, outer_db, kind (loop, soundscape-stem, easter-egg, spoken), title. Spoken is for Bible verses, prophetic words, life spoken over someone.
  • soundscapes: id, cloud_id, name, perfect_lat, perfect_lng, publish_at. Its stems are drops.
  • shows: id, band_id, title, starts_at, ends_at, lat, lng, radius_m, qr_code, reward_path.
  • checkins: id, show_id, user_id, checked_in_at. Written only when the person taps.
  • replies: id, drop_id or soundscape_id, author_id, body, approved_at (empty until the curator says yes).
  • follows: id, band_id, user_id, push_token, created_at.
  • Curators, bands and walkers are Supabase Auth users with a profile row that says which they are.

The audio math

Each drop has two rings: full level at the inner ring, quiet level at the outer. Between them the level slides in a straight line, in dB, so the change feels even to the ear.

For distance d from the drop, radius R, inner radius r0 (say 3 m), level L_in at r0 and L_out at R, in dB:

Every change is smoothed over 300 to 500 ms, so a stumble in the GPS sounds like a breeze. At the edge we add hysteresis: a drop switches on when you come inside R and off only when you pass R plus a few meters, so standing on the ring does not flicker it.

GPS reality. Under trees expect 5 to 15 m of error. The phone reports its accuracy with each fix, so we widen the effective radius by that number and ignore fixes worse than about 30 m. That is why an easter egg should never be smaller than about 8 m: the stone is the story, and the phone needs a circle it can find.

Multi-stem soundscapes. Each stem is its own drop around the perfect spot. Stand there and every stem sits at its inner level, so you hear the mix as it sounds on the record, in stereo. Walk toward the rock and the violin comes up. Stereo here means the mix at the spot. All stems run together and only the gains move, so nothing drifts. Head tracking with AirPods is a later option, on Apple's PHASE framework: trail B.

Realities to respect

  • Background audio and location on iOS: native yes, web no.
  • File sizes and offline. Masters stay WAV in Storage. The app downloads AAC at 128 to 192 kbps, about 1 to 1.5 MB a minute, and caches the whole park before you leave Wi-Fi, since the park may have no signal.
  • Replies are moderated (my addition). Nobody else hears a reply until the curator approves it.
  • Privacy (my addition). We store only what the experience needs. The phone downloads the cloud's drop list and measures distance on the device. All it sends is the check-in a person taps, and the anonymous return signal from trail L if we build it. No location history leaves the phone, and no analytics follow a person.
  • The phone-in-hand tension. One tap to begin, then the screen locks and the app keeps going.
  • App Store rules. The background modes must be used for what they say: a plain reason at the prompt, and real sound playing in the background.
  • Radii must fit the ground (my addition). Thirty feet is about 9 m, inside GPS error under trees. Expect 15 to 30 m for loops; keep small radii for open ground.

What it costs

  • Apple Developer Program: 99 USD a year. Whoever owns this account owns the app, so decide that together.
  • Supabase: the org you already use is on Pro, so this fits inside it as a schema and adds nothing. A project of its own would add about 10 USD a month.
  • Domain: about 15 USD a year.
  • Map tiles: free at this scale.
  • TestFlight: free.
  • Total: about 115 USD a year inside the existing Supabase project, or about 235 with its own, before anyone is paid for time.
